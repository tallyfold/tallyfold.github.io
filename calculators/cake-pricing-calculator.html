What goes into the price of a cake

Most home bakers start from the ingredients and double them. That leaves out the largest cost, which is nearly always time. A cake with £9.50 of ingredients and two and a half hours of work has cost £44.50 before the oven is switched on, if an hour of your time is worth £14.

  1. Ingredients. What went into this cake, not what the whole bag cost. 300 g from a 1.5 kg bag of flour at £1.10 is 22p.
  2. Your time. The hours you were working on it, at a rate you would accept from an employer. Time in the oven does not count unless you could do nothing else.
  3. Overheads. Energy, tins and tools that wear out, insurance, registration costs, a website. Add up a month of these and divide by the number of cakes you make in a month.
  4. Packaging. The box, the board, dowels, ribbon, and the fuel if you deliver.
  5. Profit. What is left for the business after you have been paid for your time: money for a slow month, a new mixer, a cake that has to be made twice.

The worked example

£9.50 of ingredients, 2.5 hours at £14 (£35.00), £3.00 of overheads and £2.20 of packaging come to a cost of £49.70. To keep 30% of the price as profit, divide the cost by 0.70: the price is £71.00, of which £21.30 is profit. Cut into 16, that is £4.44 a serving.

Margin is not markup

This calculator asks for profit as a share of the price, which is a margin. Adding 30% to the cost is a markup, and it gives a lower price: £64.61 here, not £71.00. The markup and margin calculator turns one into the other.

What the answer is, and is not

It is the price below which this cake does not pay you what you asked for. It is not a forecast of what customers will pay. If the figure is well above what cakes like yours sell for locally, the usual causes are hours that could be shortened, or a rate set for decorating work being applied to simple bakes. If it is well below, you have room.
